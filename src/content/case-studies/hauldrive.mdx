Decision 14 in the log says permissions live in the access token. Decision 38,
written about ten months later, says they do not. Both were right when they were
made, and the distance between them is most of this write-up.

## What the thing actually does

UK transport operators are legally obliged to prove their vehicles are
roadworthy. Not to believe it: to prove it, with dated records, on demand. Every
vehicle has a preventive maintenance inspection on a cycle measured in weeks.
Brake tests have their own schedule. Drivers must complete a walkaround check
before each shift, and any defect they find starts a clock. Miss enough of this
and an operator loses the licence the business runs on.

Hauldrive is the system that runs that. It holds vehicles, drivers, operating
centres, walkaround checks, defects, work orders, maintenance templates,
incidents, insurance, driver training, toolbox talks and holidays, and it has to
be able to answer "prove it" for any of them, for any date, months later.

I joined to build the backend and have led every generation of it, with a team
of four: the original Go service, the domain-separated rewrite, and the current
split into five services. As of November 2026 it is live with four operators,
100 drivers and 120+ vehicles.

## The shape now

Five services behind one gateway, plus one that users never reach:

- **identity** owns the account and nothing else. Organisations, users, members,
  sessions, staff accounts, product entitlement, terms acceptance. It issues
  every token and publishes JWKS. No roles, no billing: everything logs in
  through it, so it stays small.
- **platform** owns the commercial plane. Pricing, trials, billing, invoices,
  Stripe, audit.
- **fleet** is the original product, relocated wholesale.
- **workshop** is the second product, sold to the garages those fleets already
  book into.
- **tacho** handles tachograph data.
- **documents** is an internal file service with no gateway route at all,
  reachable only from inside the platform.

That is 600+ endpoints and 192 migrations across the service databases, in a Go
workspace with one shared module for the pieces everything needs.

## Not breaking the clients

The hard part of a split is never the split. It is that three front-ends and a
driver mobile app are calling the old URLs, the mobile app updates on its users'
schedule rather than yours, and none of that can stop.

The obvious approach is to have each service answer both its new path and the
old one it inherited. It works, and it means every service carries a second
contract for however long it takes the last client to update, which is forever,
because nothing forces the last client to update.

Instead the gateway rewrites. Traefik routes by path prefix to the service that
owns it, and the legacy prefixes are rewritten there:

| Prefix | Goes to |
|---|---|
| `/api/v1/identity/*` | identity |
| `/api/v1/fleet/org/*` | fleet, manager surface |
| `/api/v1/fleet/driver/*` | fleet, driver surface |
| `/api/v1/account/*` | **legacy**, rewritten to identity |
| `/api/v1/org/*`, `/api/v1/driver/*` | **legacy**, rewritten to fleet |

The services only ever know their own contract. The compatibility lives in one
routing file, the same file production reads, and it can be deleted the day
telemetry shows nothing hitting those prefixes. One awkward case earns its
complexity: `PUT`/`PATCH /api/v1/org` goes to identity because the company
record is identity's row, while `GET /api/v1/org` stays with fleet. That one is
matched on method.

No client changed. That was the requirement, and it is the part I would defend
hardest in a review.

## The decision I reversed

In a single service with one database, putting the permission list in the access
token is a good trade. Authorization costs a signature check and an array scan
instead of three joins on every request, and the staleness window is bounded by
the token lifetime, which you control. Rebuild the claim on every refresh and a
permission change takes effect within fifteen minutes with no logout and no
cache to invalidate. That is defensible, and I would do it again in that
situation.

It stopped being defensible the moment identity was the only thing minting
tokens.

Once authorization lives in a claim, identity is asserting something about a
domain it does not own. Fleet knows what a fleet manager may do. Workshop knows
what a workshop foreman may do. Identity knows neither, and should not, because
the second it does, every permission change in any product becomes a deploy of
the one service everything logs in through.

There is a worse version. With one service, the staleness window was bounded
because refresh and authorization were the same code path. With five they are
not. A token minted by identity gets presented to fleet, which has no idea
whether the grants inside it still exist in fleet's own tables. The claim
outlives its own revocation and nothing is positioned to notice.

I tried to save the pattern first. Put a permissions version in the token, bump
a counter in Redis when roles change, have each service compare. It works. I had
it running for about a week. Then I deleted it, because it makes every service
depend on a shared Redis for authorization correctness, which is exactly the
coupling the split existed to remove, and it leaves two sources of truth that can
drift.

So the token carries identity and nothing else. Each service resolves
permissions from its own tables at request time, against a catalogue it owns:
133 permission keys defined in Go rather than stored in a table, so a missing
grant fails the build instead of a seed script at three in the morning.

The cost is a database read in front of every endpoint. Exactly the thing the
original design existed to avoid, and it turns out to be cheap, because it is one
indexed read against the service's own database rather than the three-join
traversal I was picturing when I wrote decision 14. I had been comparing against
the wrong number for a year.

One claim survived. Ownership is `organization_members.is_owner`, identity's
column, minted as an `org_admin` claim. An owner passes fleet's permission checks
without holding any role. It is the exception because it is the one fact about
authorization that genuinely belongs to identity.

## Consistency without foreign keys

Separate databases mean the database stops enforcing referential integrity for
you, and the usual answer is to pretend that is fine until it is not.

Three pieces replace it. A transactional outbox on publish, so an event and the
row that caused it commit together or not at all. An idempotent inbox on
consume, so a redelivery is a no-op rather than a duplicate. And a nightly
reconcile that repairs whatever neither caught, because something always gets
through and the alternative is discovering it from a customer.

Events go over RabbitMQ. Direct service-to-service calls go over gRPC on
Protocol Buffers contracts rather than services calling each other's REST APIs:
identity provisioning and terms, platform billing, documents, with generated
clients in the shared module. Clients still get REST through the gateway, so the
public contract never had to change to suit an internal one.

Those internal calls authenticate with a per-call Ed25519 JWT against pinned
keys. That is what lets the documents service refuse every user token that
exists and be reachable only from inside the platform.

## Billing, and why not Stripe Subscriptions

Hauldrive charges per vehicle, per month. An operator adds nine vehicles on
onboarding day and three more the following week. One goes off the road in March
and comes back in April. The quantity is a consequence of the customer using the
product, not a number anyone typed at checkout.

Subscription APIs model a thing the customer bought. This needed to model a
thing the customer is doing, so the engine is ours and Stripe is a payment
gateway and invoice renderer: an activity ledger rather than a counter,
immutable cycles, base fee in advance with usage in arrears, mid-cycle proration
through the ledger, BACS mandates, versioned trial policies and dunning.

The test for whether this was the right call is simple. When support asks why an
account was charged for 41 vehicles in March, it is one query against one
database, not two systems and a webhook log.

## The AI part, which mostly does not decide anything

Parts and labour invoices arrive as PDFs and photographs. Extracting them by
hand is the kind of work that quietly consumes a person.

The pipeline tiers OCR into LLM extraction, so the cheap path handles clean
scans and the expensive one runs only when it has to. Supplier and part names
are resolved against records already in the database rather than trusted as
typed. Anti-fraud checks flag a suspect line for review.

Flag, not block. The model annotates and a person decides, because this is money
and an extraction that is confidently wrong is worse than one that admits doubt.

## Trade-offs

**The split cost more than it saves, today.** Four operators do not need five
services. It was done for the second product and the third, and on a four-operator
load a monolith would be cheaper to run and easier to debug. That is a bet on
where the product goes, and it is worth saying out loud rather than pretending
the architecture is free.

Identity and fleet still share one database. That is a deliberate step in the
plan, not an oversight, and it is documented as such. Separating them is its own
piece of work with its own risks, and doing it before there was a reason would
have been motion rather than progress.

The nightly reconcile is a confession. In a single database those rows could not
have drifted. Now they can, and the reconcile is the price.

Go 1.26.2 is pinned hard, because the race detector rebuilds the standard
library and will not mix two toolchains. Anything older builds fine and then
fails in CI, which is a bad way to find out.

## Where it is now

**Update, November 2026:** the split went to production on 25 October 2026,
and the platform now serves four operators, 100 drivers and 120+ vehicles. The
rest of this section is the state at the time of writing.

In production with four operators. Fleet, identity and documents run; platform
is scaffolding; workshop and tacho are modules with the shape visible and the
code still coming.

There is a decision log, past 150 entries now, including the ones that were reversed
and the reasoning that reversed them. It is the artefact I would point at first
if someone asked what working with me is like, because the useful thing is
rarely the decision. It is knowing why, and being able to tell when it stops
being true.
