Three quotes for the same eight-page product: Rs 45,000, Rs 2.8 lakh, and Rs 11 lakh. The founder assumed the expensive one was padding and the cheap one was a bargain. Both assumptions were wrong, and the reason is that she was not comparing three prices. She was comparing three completely different things.

I am one of these options, so read the rest knowing that. I have tried to be useful rather than flattering, which mostly means telling you when the answer is not me.

## The four options

- **An agency.** You get a team, a project manager, a process, and a contract with a company rather than a person. In India that runs roughly Rs 2,500 to Rs 6,000 an hour, or six figures upward for a project.

- **An independent engineer.** One experienced person who owns the whole thing. Roughly Rs 80,000 to Rs 4,00,000 for a first build, depending enormously on who.

- **The lowest bid on a marketplace.** Rs 400 to Rs 900 an hour. Sometimes an excellent engineer who has not worked out their pricing. Sometimes not a single person at all.

- **Your first engineering hire.** Three to six months to fill, then a salary, plus the part nobody budgets: you now manage someone, and if you are not technical you cannot evaluate their work.

## What actually separates them

Not skill. The distribution of talent overlaps far more than price suggests. What differs is who carries which risk.

An agency sells you continuity. If the developer on your project quits, that is their problem, not yours. That is a real thing to buy and it is most of what the premium is for. What you give up is that you meet a senior engineer at the pitch and get assigned whoever is free in delivery, with an account manager in the middle of every question. That is not dishonesty, it is how a bench works.

An independent sells you the opposite trade. The person you talked to is the person who writes the code, so nothing is lost in translation and decisions get made by someone who understands your domain. The risk is entirely concentrated: if they disappear, get ill, or take a full-time job, you are stuck. Mitigate that with your code in your repository from day one and milestones short enough that you never have more than a few weeks of work at risk.

A hire is the right answer when the work is permanent, and a slow expensive answer when it is not. The question is not "do I need a developer", it is "will there still be eight weeks of work every month in a year". If yes, hire. If the honest answer is that you have one project and then maintenance, hiring is the wrong instrument.

## The cheap bid

I am at the lower end of this market myself, so let me be careful here.

Cheap is not the problem. Unaccountable is.

A low quote from a named person who will show you a production system with real users on it, agree a written scope and hand you the repository is a genuinely good deal. Someone early in their career pricing below market to build a reference is one of the few real arbitrages available to a founder, and you should take it when you find it.

A low quote from an anonymous profile with a portfolio of screenshots and no live URL is the *expensive* option. You pay once to build it and once more to have it unpicked, and the second invoice is always larger. The test is never the number. It is whether you can verify anything they told you.

## How to actually decide

Four questions, in order.

Is this work permanent? If yes, hire, and use a contractor to get you to the point where the hire has something to work on.

Is the spec written down? If not, no quote you receive is comparable to any other, because each person is filling the gaps with different assumptions. That is where the ten-times spread comes from. A page of written scope, listing what is explicitly out as well as in, costs you an hour and is the highest-return hour in the process.

What happens if this person vanishes? Ask it out loud. The answer determines whether you need an agency's continuity or whether short milestones and owning your own repository is enough.

Is it high-stakes and domain-heavy? Payments, compliance, anything touching money or the law. Here experience with the *specific* problem matters more than anything else on this list, and it is worth paying a premium for someone who has shipped it before, whatever shape that person comes in.

## When I would tell you not to hire me

Because a page like this is worthless if it only argues one way.

If you need a team of four working in parallel to hit a date, I am one person and the date will not move for me.

If the work is genuinely permanent, hire someone. I will happily build the thing and hand it over, but a contractor is a bad substitute for a first engineer.

If you need a native mobile app in Swift or Kotlin, I do not build those.

If you want the cheapest possible build and plan to rebuild it properly later, take the cheapest bid. You are buying a throwaway and you should pay throwaway prices. I would rather not be part of that plan.

And if you already have an engineer you trust who is simply overloaded, the answer is usually to take work off them rather than to add a second opinion they did not ask for.
