A founder I spoke to had three quotes for the same eight-page product: Rs 45,000, Rs 2.8 lakh, and Rs 11 lakh. Same brief, same feature list, same deadline. Nobody had explained the spread, so she assumed the expensive one was padding and the cheap one was a bargain.

Both assumptions were wrong, and the post exists because the spread is real and nobody writes it down.

## The four bands

Roughly what exists in the Indian market right now, for a first version of a software product that real users will touch.

| Band | Typical quote | What you are buying |
|---|---|---|
| Marketplace bid | Rs 25,000 to Rs 80,000 | One person, no process, code you probably cannot extend |
| Independent engineer | Rs 80,000 to Rs 4,00,000 | One experienced person who owns the whole thing |
| Small studio | Rs 4,00,000 to Rs 12,00,000 | A team, a project manager, and overhead you fund |
| Established agency | Rs 12,00,000 upward | The above plus a sales function and a brand |

Those bands overlap and the labels lie constantly. A "studio" can be one person subcontracting. A marketplace profile can be a genuinely excellent engineer who has not worked out their pricing yet. What matters is not the label, it is the four things below.

## What actually moves the number

Scope is the smallest factor, which surprises people. A login screen is a login screen. The things that move a quote by a factor of ten:

Who writes the code, and whether you meet them. In the studio and agency bands you meet a senior engineer during the pitch and get assigned whoever is free in delivery. That is not dishonesty, it is how a bench works, and it is the single biggest difference between the Rs 3 lakh quote and the Rs 11 lakh one. The Rs 11 lakh buys you insulation from that risk. Sometimes.

Whether anyone has built this specific thing before. Payments, multi-tenancy, compliance workflows and anything touching money are where estimates fall apart, because the hard part is not the happy path. Someone who has shipped one before quotes lower and delivers faster than someone who is about to learn on your budget.

Who carries the risk of being wrong. A fixed price means the builder eats the overrun. An hourly rate means you do. Fixed prices are therefore always higher than the honest hourly estimate, because the number includes insurance. That premium is usually worth paying for a first build, when you are the one least able to absorb a surprise.

What happens after launch. Most cheap quotes exclude it entirely, and it is not in the document you signed. Ask directly: who fixes it at 2am in week three, and is that included?

## The cheap quote is not the problem

I want to be careful here, because I am at the lower end of these bands myself and it would be self-serving to argue cheap is bad.

Cheap is not the problem. Unaccountable is.

A Rs 45,000 quote from a named person who will show you a production system with real users on it, agree a written scope, and hand you the repository is a genuinely good deal, and someone early in their career pricing below market to build a reference is one of the few real arbitrages available to a founder.

A Rs 45,000 quote from an anonymous profile with a portfolio of screenshots and no live URL is the expensive option. You pay once to build it and once more to have it unpicked, and the second invoice is always larger than the first.

The test is not the number. The test is whether you can check anything they told you.

## Questions that separate the two

Ask these on the first call. The answers take a minute each and they sort the field fast.

**Show me something running with real users on it.** Not a demo, not a screenshot, not a Figma file. A URL, or a company name I can look up. Anyone who has shipped can do this in ten seconds. Anyone who cannot has an explanation, and the explanation is the answer.

**Who owns the code, and where does it live from day one?** The correct answer is: you do, in your repository, on your infrastructure, from the first commit. If the code lives somewhere only they can log into until final payment, you are not buying software, you are renting a hostage.

Then the three that expose padding in either direction:

- What would you cut first if we had half the budget? A good answer is specific and slightly painful. A bad one is "nothing, the scope is minimal".
- What is the part of this you are least sure about? Everyone has one. Someone who claims not to has either done this exact thing before, in which case ask to see it, or has not thought about it.
- What happens if it takes twice as long as you think? You are listening for whether they have a mechanism, not a promise.

## What I charge, and why it is low

Since I have opinions about everyone else's pricing, here is mine.

Projects start at Rs 8,000 for a focused piece of work, an MVP build starts around Rs 20,000, and open-ended work is Rs 12,000 a week. That is below every band in the table, including the marketplace one, and I want to be straightforward about why rather than pretend it reflects some efficiency nobody else has found.

I am early in freelancing. I have production systems behind me: a fleet compliance platform four UK operators run their business on, and a game a million people registered for. What I do not yet have is a list of freelance clients who will take a call and vouch for me. That list is worth more to me right now than the margin I am giving up to build it, so the first few engagements are priced to be easy to say yes to.

The honest consequence: these numbers go up once that list exists. Anything agreed before then holds for the length of the engagement.

## If you take one thing

Get the scope written down before you compare prices at all.

Almost every ten-times spread I have seen came from three people quoting three different products, because the brief was a paragraph and each of them filled the gaps with their own assumptions. The cheap quote assumed no admin panel. The expensive one assumed SSO and an audit log. Nobody was lying and nobody was comparable.

A page of written scope, listing what is explicitly out as well as in, turns three incomparable numbers into three comparable ones. It costs an hour. It is the highest-return hour in the whole process, and you can write it yourself before you speak to anyone.
